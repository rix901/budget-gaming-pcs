## What $500–$600 actually buys right now

The honest framing: entry-level prices are worse than they were a year ago, and it's not a marketing problem. New RX 6600 cards sit around $239. A 1TB NVMe SSD that ran about $45 in late 2025 is closer to $90. The RAM kit is the biggest swing.

## Three routes that actually work

### Route 1: A new prebuilt, accepting integrated graphics

If you want a box that arrives, boots into Windows and carries a warranty, the realistic sub-$600 option uses an APU rather than a separate card. The chip to look for is the Ryzen 5 5600GT, a 6-core/12-thread part with Radeon Vega 7 graphics built in. Configs with 16GB DDR4 and a 512GB NVMe have been listed around $569.99 at Newegg against a much higher list price.

Set expectations properly: Vega 7 lands somewhere between an old GT 1030 and a GTX 1050. Esports titles run fine at 1080p low-to-medium. Big AAA games will launch, sometimes only with upscaling on, often under 60 FPS. The compensation is a free PCIe slot, so a GPU upgrade later is a five-minute job.

Prebuilt towers with real cards do show up on deal. Woot has run entry-level CyberPowerPC Gamer Master systems around the $495 mark with a stackable promo code — Ryzen 5 5500 plus Radeon RX 6500 XT, 8GB RAM, 500GB SSD, keyboard and mouse in the box. That's a genuine starter machine for 1080p esports, with RAM and storage as obvious first upgrades. Similar towers from STGAubron with an RTX 2060 and 16GB have appeared near $589.

### Route 2: DIY, potentially with a used GPU

Building from parts gets you a newer platform and a clear upgrade path. A consensus 2026 budget list looks like this:

Add a Windows licence, roughly $120–$140, unless you already own one. That's the trap in DIY math: a $500 parts list becomes a $640 build once Windows is in.

What that build does: Valorant and CS2 at 120–180 FPS on high, Fortnite around 80–100 FPS on medium, Cyberpunk 2077 at 35–45 FPS on low. Nothing here is a 4K machine. The AM4 platform is the real prize, since a much faster CPU drops straight in later.

### Route 3: A refurbished office tower plus a used GPU

This is the cheapest path to real gaming frames and it looks the least impressive. A refurbished OptiPlex 7060 MT with an i7-8700 and 16GB typically runs $150–$200. Pair it with a used slot-powered GTX 1650 (~$100–$130) or a used RX 6600 (~$200–$260) and you land in the $350–$420 range with performance that beats most sub-$500 new prebuilts.

Two checks before you buy: take the mini-tower version rather than the small form factor, since low-profile GPUs cost more and there are fewer of them, and confirm the listing includes a Windows licence.

## The fine print that decides whether you get paid

Confirmation windows of 85 to 120 days are normal, and they're the main reason people think cashback is unreliable. It isn't mysterious, but it is fragile. These are the things that break tracking:

- **Leaving the session.** Visiting another coupon or referral site between the click-through and checkout hands the referral elsewhere. Reactivate through ShopBack if that happens.
- **Outside promo codes.** A code found somewhere other than the platform can carry its own affiliate attribution and override the referral. Use the codes ShopBack surfaces.
- **Ad-blockers and strict tracking protection.** These can block the affiliate cookie or the confirmation ping. Allow-list the retailer during checkout, or finish the purchase in a clean browser.
- **In-app browsers.** The mini browser inside a social or messaging app often drops the cookie. Open the link in your normal browser or the app.
- **Pre-existing carts.** Newegg, HP and Lenovo all require an empty cart before you click through — items already sitting in the cart won't track. This one catches people constantly.
- **Excluded line items.** Gift cards, sales tax and shipping are commonly excluded from the qualifying amount, and each store page lists its own exclusions.

Two more practical notes. Keep your entire purchase on one device from start to finish, and don't buy from a storefront aimed at another country, since cross-market orders are typically rejected.

If something doesn't show as tracked within 48 hours, file a missing cashback claim with the order number, date and amount. Support checks tracking records against the merchant's data.

## Questions that come up

**Is a $500 gaming PC worth it at all?**
Depends on what you play. For esports and older AAA titles at 1080p, yes — a refurb tower plus a used RX 6600 gets you there for around $400. For current AAA at high settings, no. The $600–$700 band is where that starts working, and the jump from $900 to $1,900 delivers far less than the jump from $500 to $900.

**Prebuilt or DIY?**
DIY wins on frames per dollar and upgrade path if you're willing to spend an afternoon on it. Prebuilts win on warranty, Windows licensing and not having to debug a no-POST board at 11pm. At this price point, DIY is usually $60–$100 cheaper for the same GPU class, not dramatically so.

**What should I check first in a prebuilt listing?**
The GPU model, by name. "Gaming i7" with no GPU listed almost always means integrated graphics. A Ryzen 5 paired with a named GTX 1660 Super beats an i7 with Intel UHD at the same price every time. After the GPU, look at the PSU brand — unrated 500W units in sub-$650 prebuilts cause reboots under load.

**How do I know the cashback rate is what I think it is?**
Open the retailer's page on the platform and read the current rate and the exclusions on that page. Rates get tracked at an initial value and adjusted when the transaction confirms, so a pending figure can move.

**Do I need to install anything?**
No. The site and the app both work. There's also a browser extension for Chrome, Edge and Firefox that surfaces the current rate and activates cashback when you land on a partner retailer — useful if you're the type who forgets to start the trip from the platform.
